According to BleepingComputer, Ukrainian authorities working with the FBI (Federal Bureau of Investigation) arrested four suspected members of an international ransomware organization. The announcement was published on November 18, 2026. The key members were notified of suspicion under Part 2 of Article 255 and Part 5 of Article 361 of the Criminal Code of Ukraine and placed in detention, and they face up to 15 years in prison.

## The organization

The criminal organization includes at least 27 individuals from various countries, including Ukraine, Kazakhstan, and Russia. The wider network associated with it has over 60 members in eight jurisdictions, among them former members of the Conti hacker group.

## How the intrusions worked

The group created its own platform featuring voice messages generated by artificial intelligence (AI), and called company employees while posing as IT support specialists. Through that approach, members obtained credentials and persuaded employees to install remote access programs, with AI used to automate fraudulent calls. In one documented episode, 1.4 terabytes of data were stolen from an American enterprise; the attackers demanded 10 million US dollars to restore access and, after negotiations, received 5 million dollars.

## Why it matters in production

The case is larger than the arrests: at least 28 companies paid cybercriminals over 40.5 million USD. Those victims were enterprises in the oil and gas, pharmaceutical, industrial, and logistics industries, financial institutions, and critical infrastructure facilities.

## The investigation

The activities of the organization were documented for more than two years by officers of the Cyber Police Department and investigators of the Main Investigation Department, in cooperation with the FBI. As part of the operation, 66 simultaneous searches were conducted in Kyiv and 11 regions of Ukraine.

## What to do

This is a criminal case rather than a product advisory, so the detail to act on is the intrusion method the sources describe. Staff should be able to check an unsolicited IT-support call before installing anything, and someone should own the approval of remote-access installs.
